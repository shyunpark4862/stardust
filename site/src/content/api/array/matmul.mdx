`matmul` computes the matrix product: for `x1` with $n$ rows and $k$ columns and `x2` with $k$ rows and $m$
columns, element `(i, j)` of the result is the sum over `p` of `x1[i, p] * x2[p, j]`. The operator `x1 @ x2` calls
`matmul`, and `x1 @= x2` stores the product in `x1`.

A 1-D operand acts as a vector: on the left as a matrix with one row, on the right as a matrix with one column. The
axis added for it is removed from the result, so a matrix times a vector is a vector, and a vector times a vector is
their inner product, returned as a Python scalar.

Arrays with more than two dimensions are stacks of matrices: the last two axes hold each matrix, and the axes before
them index the stack. The stack axes of the two operands broadcast against each other, so one matrix multiplies every
matrix of a stack.

The result dtype follows [type promotion](/api/array/dtypes/#type-promotion), except that the product of two `bool`
arrays is `int64`: the products are summed, not combined with logical or (see
[arithmetic on Booleans](/api/array/dtypes/#arithmetic-on-booleans)). Integer products and sums wrap around
outside the `int64` range (see [integer overflow](/api/array/dtypes/#integer-overflow)). Complex operands are not
conjugated; [`vecdot`](/api/array/vecdot/) conjugates its first argument.

## Examples

The product of two matrices, with the function and with the operator:

```python
>>> import stardust as sd
>>> a = sd.asarray([[1, 2], [3, 4]])
>>> b = sd.asarray([[5, 6], [7, 8]])
>>> sd.matmul(a, b)
array([[19, 22],
       [43, 50]], dtype=int64)
>>> a @ b
array([[19, 22],
       [43, 50]], dtype=int64)
```

A matrix times a vector, a vector times a matrix, and the inner product of two vectors:

```python
>>> v = sd.asarray([1.0, -1.0])
>>> a @ v
array([-1.0, -1.0], dtype=float64)
>>> v @ a
array([-2.0, -2.0], dtype=float64)
>>> v @ v
2.0
```

One matrix multiplies each matrix of a stack. Stack axes of different lengths broadcast:

```python
>>> s = sd.reshape(sd.arange(8), (2, 2, 2))
>>> s @ a
array([[[ 3,  4],
        [11, 16]],

       [[19, 28],
        [27, 40]]], dtype=int64)
>>> (sd.ones((3, 1, 2, 4)) @ sd.ones((5, 4, 6))).shape
(3, 5, 2, 6)
```

Booleans give `int64`, and `@=` stores the product in the left operand when the shape and dtype allow it:

```python
>>> t = sd.asarray([[True, False], [True, True]])
>>> t @ t
array([[1, 0],
       [2, 1]], dtype=int64)
>>> f = sd.asarray([[1.0, 2.0], [3.0, 4.0]])
>>> f @= sd.asarray([[0.0, 1.0], [1.0, 0.0]])
>>> f
array([[2.0, 1.0],
       [4.0, 3.0]], dtype=float64)
```

The inner lengths must agree, and scalars are not operands of `matmul`:

```python
>>> sd.matmul(a, sd.ones((3, 2)))
Traceback (most recent call last):
    ...
ValueError: matmul(): the last axis of 'x1' (2) must match the second-to-last axis of 'x2' (3)
>>> a @ 2
Traceback (most recent call last):
    ...
TypeError: unsupported operand type(s) for @: 'ndarray' and 'int'
```

## Notes

For matrices $A$ of shape $(n, k)$ and $B$ of shape $(k, m)$, the product $C = AB$ has shape $(n, m)$ and

$$
C_{ij} = \sum_{p=0}^{k-1} A_{ip} B_{pj} .
$$

With $k = 0$, every element is the empty sum 0. NaN and infinity follow the arithmetic of each product and sum, so
`nan * 0.0` contributes NaN.

`x1 @= x2` computes the product and stores it into `x1`. The product must have the shape of `x1`, so `x2` is a
square matrix or a stack of them, and its dtype must convert to that of `x1` (see
[implicit conversion](/api/array/dtypes/#implicit-conversion)). Otherwise the operator raises `ValueError` (shape)
or `TypeError` (dtype) and leaves `x1` unchanged.

Linear systems are solved with [`sd.linalg.solve`](/api/linalg/solve/), not by multiplying with an inverse.

## Comparison with other libraries

The nearest equivalents are `numpy.matmul` and the `@` operator of NumPy arrays.

- Both operands must be `sd.ndarray`; NumPy also accepts lists. Python scalars raise `TypeError` here and
  `ValueError` in NumPy.
- The product of two `bool` arrays is `int64`. NumPy returns `bool`.
- There is no `out=` argument, and no `dot` or `inner` function.
